Non-Hohmann Transfers with a Common Apse Line

The Hohmann transfer requires that the spacecraft have the thrust impulses applied at periapsis and apoapsis of the transfer orbit. However, this limits the possible timing of the transfer if we are trying to intercept another celestial body.

Instead, it would be more convenient if we could transfer to or from any point in the initial or target orbit. This would require more Δv\Delta vΔv than a Hohmann transfer, but gives more flexibility. The only required condition is that the transfer orbit must intersect both the initial and final orbits.

For now, we will assume that the initial and target orbits have a common apse line. In addition, the two orbits will have the same focus. Assume that we want to leave the initial orbit at the true anomaly νA\nu_AνA​ and arrive at the target orbit at the true anomaly νB\nu_BνB​.

Since the two orbits have the same focus, the transfer orbit will also have the same focus. In addition, since the apse lines are in common, the departure and arrival true anomalies are the same in the transfer orbit. This situation is shown in Fig. 73.

Therefore, we can apply the orbit equation at the departure and arrival points:

rA=pt1+etcos⁡νArB=pt1+etcos⁡νB\begin{aligned} r_A &= \frac{p_t}{1 + e_t\cos\nu_A} & r_B &= \frac{p_t}{1 + e_t\cos\nu_B} \end{aligned}rA​​=1+et​cosνA​pt​​​rB​​=1+et​cosνB​pt​​​
(342)

where rAr_ArA​ and rBr_BrB​ are the orbital radii at the departure and arrival points. Note that ptp_tpt​ is the orbital parameter for the transfer orbit, and p=h2/μp = h^2/\mup=h2/μ.

Now we have two equations and two unknowns for the transfer orbit, ete_tet​ and ptp_tpt​. Solving for these two, we find:

et=rB−rArAcos⁡νA−rBcos⁡νBpt=rArBcos⁡νA−cos⁡νBrAcos⁡νA−rBcos⁡νB\begin{aligned} e_t &= \frac{r_B - r_A}{r_A \cos\nu_A - r_B\cos\nu_B} \\ p_t &= r_A r_B\frac{\cos\nu_A - \cos\nu_B}{r_A\cos\nu_A - r_B\cos\nu_B} \end{aligned}et​pt​​=rA​cosνA​−rB​cosνB​rB​−rA​​=rA​rB​rA​cosνA​−rB​cosνB​cosνA​−cosνB​​​

Calculating Δv\Delta vΔv¶

For orbital transfers that do not take place on the apse line, we have to account for the change in direction of the velocity vector in addition to the change of magnitude. The difference in length of the velocity vectors is the change in speed and the change of flight path angle is the change of direction of the vector.

It is very important that when we calculate the required Δv\Delta vΔv for a maneuver, this is the magnitude of the change in velocity vector, not the change of magnitude of the velocity (speed). Since Δ𝒗=𝒗At−𝒗A\Delta\vector{v} = \vector{v}_{A_t} - \vector{v}_AΔv=vAt​​−vA​, the magnitude is:

Δv=∥Δ𝒗∥=(𝒗At−𝒗A)⋅(𝒗At−𝒗A)\Delta v = \mag{\Delta\vector{v}} = \sqrt{\left(\vector{v}_{A_t} - \vector{v}_{A}\right)\cdot\left(\vector{v}_{A_t} - \vector{v}_{A}\right)}Δv=∥Δv∥=(vAt​​−vA​)⋅(vAt​​−vA​)​
(345)

Expanding the product and simplifying with the law of cosines, we find:

Δv=vA2+vAt2−2vAvAtcos⁡Δϕ\Delta v = \sqrt{v_A^2 + v_{A_t}^2 - 2 v_A v_{A_t} \cos\Delta\phi}Δv=vA2​+vAt​2​−2vA​vAt​​cosΔϕ​
(346)

where Δϕ=ϕAt−ϕA\Delta\phi = \phi_{A_t} - \phi_AΔϕ=ϕAt​​−ϕA​ is the change of flight path angle. The flight path angle is given by Eq. 128.

This situation is shown in Fig. 74 for the departure point.

This gives the required velocity change at the departure point, AAA. The same calculation can be done at the arrival point, BBB, to compute the total Δv\Delta vΔv for the maneuver.

Thrust Direction¶

To achieve the new velocity, the thrust from the engine must be directed parallel to the direction of Δ𝒗\Delta\vector{v}Δv. Relative to the local horizon, the required angle γ\gammaγ is given by:

tan⁡γ=ΔvrΔv⊥\tan\gamma = \frac{\Delta v_r}{\Delta v_{\perp}}tanγ=Δv⊥​Δvr​​
(347)

where Δvr\Delta v_rΔvr​ is the change in the radial component of velocity and Δv⊥\Delta v_{\perp}Δv⊥​ is the change in the perpendicular component of velocity.

Relation to Specific Energy¶

The vis viva equation determines the specific energy of the spacecraft due to its kinetic and potential energy by Eq. 95. The impulsive maneuver in this transfer changes the velocity but not the position (by definition). Then, we can calculate the change in specific energy due to the impulse:

ΔE=(𝒗+Δ𝒗)⋅(𝒗+Δ𝒗)2−𝒗⋅𝒗2\Delta E = \frac{\left(\vector{v} + \Delta\vector{v}\right)\cdot\left(\vector{v} + \Delta\vector{v}\right)}{2} - \frac{\vector{v}\cdot\vector{v}}{2}ΔE=2(v+Δv)⋅(v+Δv)​−2v⋅v​
(348)

Simplifying, assuming that 𝒗=𝒗A\vector{v} = \vector{v}_Av=vA​ and (𝒗+Δ𝒗)=𝒗At\left(\vector{v} + \Delta\vector{v}\right) = \vector{v}_{A_t}(v+Δv)=vAt​​, we find:

ΔE=vΔvcos⁡Δϕ+12Δv2\Delta E = v\Delta v \cos\Delta\phi + \frac{1}{2}\Delta v^2ΔE=vΔvcosΔϕ+21​Δv2
(349)

From this equation, for a fixed Δv\Delta vΔv from the maneuver, we see that ΔE\Delta EΔE is maximized when vvv is largest and Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ=0. In the sense that higher ΔE\Delta EΔE for a given Δv\Delta vΔv is more efficient, we can see that aligning the velocity vectors and maximizing vvv by providing the impulse at periapsis has the highest efficiency.
