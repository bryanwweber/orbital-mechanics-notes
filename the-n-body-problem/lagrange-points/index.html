Application of the CR3BP: Lagrange Points

The equations of motion of the circular restricted three body problem (CR3BP) were shown in Eq. 72. These non-dimensional equations of motion do not have a general analytical solution.

Lagrange himself called them libration points so you will see them called that too.

However, there are a set of five points for which the tertiary mass is in equilibrium with respect to the primary and secondary masses. An object placed at one of the equilibrium points will, if it is not perturbed, remain at that location indefinitely. These locations are called Lagrange Points.

According to the conditions for equilibrium, the acceleration and velocity components at the Lagrange points are zero:

x˙∗=y˙∗=z˙∗=0andx¨∗=y¨∗=z¨∗=0\dot{x}^* = \dot{y}^* = \dot{z}^* = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \ddot{x}^* = \ddot{y}^* = \ddot{z}^* = 0x˙∗=y˙​∗=z˙∗=0andx¨∗=y¨​∗=z¨∗=0
(73)

and likewise for the dimensional coordinates as well. Plugging these conditions in to the equations of motion, we find:

−x∗=−1−π2σ3(x∗+π2)−π2ψ3(x∗−1+π2)−y∗=−1−π2σ3y∗−π2ψ3y∗0=(−1−π2σ3−π2ψ3)z∗\begin{aligned} -x^* &= -\frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma^3}\left(x^* + \pi_2\right) - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}\left(x^* - 1 + \pi_2\right) \\ -y^* &= -\frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma^3} y^* - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}y^* \\ 0 &= \left(-\frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma^3} - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}\right)z^* \end{aligned}−x∗−y∗0​=−σ31−π2​​(x∗+π2​)−ψ3π2​​(x∗−1+π2​)=−σ31−π2​​y∗−ψ3π2​​y∗=(−σ31−π2​​−ψ3π2​​)z∗​
(74)

In the last equation, for z∗z^*z∗, the terms in the parentheses are both greater than zero. Therefore, there is no way for them to cancel each other out, and the only way for the equation to be satisfied is if z∗=0z^* = 0z∗=0. Thus, the Lagrange points lie in the orbital plane.

Now, we need to solve for the x∗x^*x∗ and y∗y^*y∗ coordinates of the Lagrange points. There are two conditions that we need to consider, since they end up with different solutions:

  1. y∗≠0y^* \neq 0y∗=0, giving the so-called equilateral Lagrange points

  2. y∗=0y^* = 0y∗=0, giving the so-called collinear Lagrange points

The collinear Lagrange points lie along the x∗x^*x∗ axis, which is a line between m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​. The equilateral Lagrange points form an equilateral triangle with m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​ as two of the corners and sides of length r12r_{12}r12​.

Equilateral Lagrange Points¶

To find the equilateral Lagrange points, we assume y∗≠0y^*\neq 0y∗=0. Now we have 2 equations and 2 unknowns. Since y∗≠0y^*\neq 0y∗=0, the y∗y^*y∗ in the second equation of motion (Eq. 74) will cancel from both sides, and we end up with:

Remember that z∗z^*z∗ is zero for all the equilibrium points.

1=1−π2σ3+π2ψ31 = \frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma_3} + \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}1=σ3​1−π2​​+ψ3π2​​
(75)

In the equation for x∗x^*x∗ in Eq. 74 we see the (1−π2)/σ3\left(1 - \pi_2\right)/\sigma^3(1−π2​)/σ3 term, so let’s solve the y∗y^*y∗ equation for that and plug into the x∗x^*x∗ equation:

x∗=(1−π2ψ3)(x∗+π2)+π2ψ3(x∗−1+π2)x^{*} = \left(1 - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}\right)\left(x^* + \pi_2\right) + \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}\left(x^* - 1 + \pi_2\right)x∗=(1−ψ3π2​​)(x∗+π2​)+ψ3π2​​(x∗−1+π2​)
(76)

Simplifying, we find:

ψ3=1\psi^3 = 1ψ3=1
(77)

Then we can use the definition of ψ\psiψ to find r2r_2r2​ in dimensional coordinates:

ψ=∥r∗2∥r∗12⇒r2=r12\psi = \frac{\mag{\vector{r}*2}}{r*{12}} \Rightarrow r_2 = r_{12}ψ=r∗12∥r∗2∥​⇒r2​=r12​
(78)

Plugging the result of Eq. 77 back into Eq. 75 equation, we find:

σ3=1\sigma^3 = 1σ3=1
(79)

Then we can use the definition of σ\sigmaσ to find r1r_1r1​ in dimensional coordinates:

σ=∥r∗1∥r∗12⇒r1=r12\sigma = \frac{\mag{\vector{r}*1}}{r*{12}} \Rightarrow r_1 = r_{12}σ=r∗12∥r∗1∥​⇒r1​=r12​
(80)

Since if a=ba = ba=b and b=cb = cb=c, then a=ca = ca=c, we find:

r1=r2=r12andψ=σr_1 = r_2 = r_{12}\qquad \text{and}\qquad \psi = \sigmar1​=r2​=r12​andψ=σ
(81)

for the equilateral Lagrange points. Eq. 81 shows that the distance from m1m_1m1​ to mmm is the same as the distance from m2m_2m2​ to mmm and from m1m_1m1​ to m2m_2m2​. This defines an equilateral triangle, giving these Lagrange points their name.

From the definition of σ\vector{\sigma}σ and ψ\vector{\psi}ψ, we can take their magnitudes and equate them to solve for the values of x∗x^*x∗ and y∗y^{*}y∗ at the equilibrium points:

σ2=∥σ∥2=(x∗+π2)2+(y∗)2ψ2=∥ψ∥2=(x∗−1+π2)2+(y∗)2\begin{aligned} \sigma^2 &= \mag{\vector{\sigma}}^2 = \left(x^{*} + \pi_2\right)^2 + \left(y^*\right)^2 \\ \psi^2 &= \mag{\vector{\psi}}^2 = \left(x^{*} - 1 + \pi_2\right)^2 + \left(y^*\right)^2 \end{aligned}σ2ψ2​=∥σ∥2=(x∗+π2​)2+(y∗)2=∥ψ∥2=(x∗−1+π2​)2+(y∗)2​
(82)

where we have also used the fact that z∗=0z^* = 0z∗=0 for the Lagrange points. Using the result from Eq. 81 that σ=ψ\sigma = \psiσ=ψ for the equilateral points, we find:

x∗=12−π2y∗=±32\begin{aligned} x^{*} &= \frac{1}{2} - \pi_2 \\ y^* &= \pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{aligned}x∗y∗​=21​−π2​=±23​​​
(83)

The equilateral Lagrange points are given the symbols L4L_4L4​ and L5L_5L5​, for the positive and negative y∗y^*y∗ values, respectively:

L4:x∗=12−π2y∗=32L5:x∗=12−π2y∗=−32\begin{aligned} &L_4: & x^* &= \frac{1}{2} - \pi_2 & y^*&= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ &L_5: & x^* &= \frac{1}{2} - \pi_2 & y^*&= -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{aligned}​L4​:L5​:​x∗x∗​=21​−π2​=21​−π2​​y∗y∗​=23​​=−23​​​
(84)

To convert these into dimensional xxx and yyy coordinates, you should multiply by r12r_{12}r12​.

Collinear Lagrange Points¶

For the collinear Lagrange points, we set y=z=y∗=z∗=0y = z = y^{*} = z^* = 0y=z=y∗=z∗=0 in the equations of motion, Eq. 72. We make this choice by inspection, seeing that setting y=y∗=0y = y^{*} = 0y=y∗=0 is one possible solution (the other case, y∗≠0y^*\neq 0y∗=0 we just handled).

This leaves only the x∗x^*x∗ equation of motion, since the other two are trivially 0=00=00=0. For x∗x^*x∗, we have from Eq. 74:

x∗=1−π2σ3(x∗+π2)+π2ψ3(x∗−1+π2)x^{*} = \frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma^3}\left(x^* + \pi_2\right) + \frac{\pi_2}{\psi^3}\left(x^* - 1 + \pi_2\right)x∗=σ31−π2​​(x∗+π2​)+ψ3π2​​(x∗−1+π2​)
(85)

The vectors σ\vector{\sigma}σ and ψ\vector{\psi}ψ are the non-dimensional versions of the vectors from m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​ to mmm.

The terms σ3\sigma^3σ3 and ψ3\psi^3ψ3 in the bottom of both terms are found by cubing the magnitude of the vectors σ\vector{\sigma}σ and ψ\vector{\psi}ψ. To find the vector magnitude, we necessarily have to take the square root, so we do not know the sign of the magnitude. Cubing a negative number will result in another negative and cubing a positive number will result in another positive.

Therefore, we do not know what the sign of σ3\sigma^3σ3 or ψ3\psi^3ψ3 will be, we have to include that as part of the solution of this equation. Cubing the magnitudes of σ\vector{\sigma}σ and psi\vector{psi}psi from Eq. 67 with y∗=0y^* = 0y∗=0 for the collinear points, we find:

σ3=∣x∗+π2∣3ψ3=∣x∗−1+π2∣3\begin{aligned} \sigma^3 &= \left\lvert x^{*} + \pi_2\right\rvert ^3 & \psi^3 &= \left\lvert x^* - 1 + \pi_2\right\rvert ^3 \end{aligned}σ3​=∣x∗+π2​∣3​ψ3​=∣x∗−1+π2​∣3​
(86)

where the single vertical lines indicate that we’re taking the absolute value. Substituting these into Eq. 85, we find:

0=x∗−1−π2∣x∗+π2∣3(x∗+π2)−π2∣x∗−1+π2∣3(x∗−1+π2)0 = x^{*} - \frac{1 - \pi_2}{\left\lvert x^* + \pi_2\right\rvert ^3}\left(x^* + \pi_2\right) - \frac{\pi_2}{\left\lvert x^* - 1 + \pi_2\right\rvert ^3}\left(x^* - 1 + \pi_2\right)0=x∗−∣x∗+π2​∣31−π2​​(x∗+π2​)−∣x∗−1+π2​∣3π2​​(x∗−1+π2​)
(87)

We cannot cancel any of the terms because we don’t know the sign of the cubic term on the bottom.

This equation is cubic in x∗x^*x∗, so it will have three separate values of x∗x^*x∗ that solve the equation, as long as 0<π2<10 < \pi_2 < 10<π2​<1. In the cases where π2=0\pi_2 = 0π2​=0 or π2=1\pi_2 = 1π2​=1, either m2m_2m2​ or m1m_1m1​ must be zero, which aren’t very interesting.

There is no analytical solution to this equation, it must be solved numerically. There are many methods to solve the equation numerically, my suggestions are to use scipy.optimize.newton() in Python and fzero in Matlab. We will see how to use these functions in the next example.

In the meantime, Fig. 15 shows the solution of Eq. 87:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import newton

plt.rc("font", size=20)


def collinear_lagrange(xstar, pi_2):
    return xstar - (1 - pi_2)/np.abs(xstar + pi_2)**3 * (xstar + pi_2) - pi_2 / np.abs(xstar - 1 + pi_2)**3 * (xstar - 1 + pi_2)


n_points = 1000
pi_2 = np.linspace(1e-5, 1.0-1e-5, n_points)
L_2 = newton(func=collinear_lagrange, x0=np.repeat(1, n_points), args=(pi_2,))
L_1 = newton(func=collinear_lagrange, x0=np.repeat(0, n_points), args=(pi_2,))
L_3 = newton(func=collinear_lagrange, x0=np.repeat(-1, n_points), args=(pi_2,))

Lagrange Point Stability¶

Although all the Lagrange points are equilibrium points, they may not be stable equilibrium points. Stability is the ability of the system to return to its initial position after being perturbed.

To analyze the stability of the Lagrange points, we will use the potential energy function for the CR3BP.

As we discussed previously, gravity is a conservative force. As such, the force field can be defined in terms of a potential energy function. For the CR3BP, the pseudo-potential function in the rotating frame is given by Koon et al. (2011):

U(x∗,y∗)=−1−π2σ−π2ψ−12[(1−π2)σ2+π2ψ2]U(x^*, y^*) = -\frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma} - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi} - \frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1 - \pi_2\right)\sigma^2 + \pi_2 \psi^2\right]U(x∗,y∗)=−σ1−π2​​−ψπ2​​−21​[(1−π2​)σ2+π2​ψ2]
(88)

A plot of this function is shown in Fig. 17, including the positions of the five Lagrange points, for π2=\pi_2 =π2​= 0.3.

Using this figure, we can get a qualitative sense of the stability of the Lagrange points. Imagine that we turn the potential function upside-down, and put a marble on each of the Lagrange points.

We can see that L4L_4L4​ and L5L_5L5​ are at the bottom of a bowl. Slight displacements of the marble will cause it to return to the initial position, so these points are considered stable Lagrange points.

On the other hand, L1L_1L1​, L2L_2L2​, and L3L_3L3​ are saddle points on the potential energy surface. This means that slight displacements will cause the object’s position to diverge from the equilibrium point over time.

The Equilateral Lagrange Points¶

Since the equilateral Lagrange points are stable, objects placed in a small orbit, usually called a halo orbit, around those points will tend to remain there. The criterion for stability is:

m1m2+m2m1≥25\frac{m_1}{m_2} + \frac{m_2}{m_1} \geq 25m2​m1​​+m1​m2​​≥25
(89)

which will be satisfied if m1/m2>24.95994m_1/m_2>24.95994m1​/m2​>24.95994 or π2<0.0385209\pi_2 < 0.0385209π2​<0.0385209. In the Earth-Moon system, that ratio is m1/m2≈81.3m_1/m_2 \approx 81.3m1​/m2​≈81.3, so L4L_4L4​ and L5L_5L5​ are nominally stable. However, the L4L_4L4​ and L5L_5L5​ points for the Earth-Moon system are slightly destabilized by the influence of the Sun and they aren’t completely stable. Nonetheless, there are clouds of dust which have collected at these points because they are kinda stable.

Other pairs of m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​ do have somewhat more stable L4L_4L4​ and L5L_5L5​ points. In particular, the orbit of Jupiter around the Sun has stable equilateral Lagrange points. There are groups of asteroids, called Trojan asteroids that cluster around the stable Lagrange points in the orbit of Jupiter, as shown in Fig. 18.

Note that Fig. 17 shows the potential surface for π2=0.3\pi_2 = 0.3π2​=0.3, which does not satisfy the numerical stability criteria here for L4L_4L4​ and L5L_5L5​ from Eq. 89. We’re plotting π2=0.3\pi_2 = 0.3π2​=0.3 to exaggerate the shape of the potential function for clarity.

The Collinear Lagrange Points¶

The collinear Lagrange points, L1L_1L1​, L2L_2L2​, and L3L_3L3​ are all saddle points in Fig. 17, meaning that the function increases when going in one axis, but decreases going in the other axis. This means that the three collinear Lagrange points are unstable and an object placed at one of those points, if perturbed, will diverge from the position.

Nonetheless, these are quite useful points for observation of the solar system. Several satellites have been placed at the L1L_1L1​ point of the Earth-Sun system for solar observation, and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is located at the L2L_2L2​ point in the Earth-Sun system specifically to avoid sunlight interfering with observations.

These satellites orbit around the unstable Lagrange points in a Lissajous orbit. This type of orbit requires a very small amount of propulsion onboard the satellite to keep position, but the orbit can last for a very long time with only a little fuel. One example is the Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) which was sent to the L2L_2L2​ point in the Earth-Sun system to study the Cosmic microwave background. Another example is the JWST, mentioned previously. JWST has a simpler halo orbit around L2L_2L2​. The orbit of JWST is shown in Fig. 20.

L1L_1L1​ and L2L_2L2​ in the Earth-Sun system are about 1.5 million km towards the Sun and away from the Sun, starting at the Earth, respectively. L3L_3L3​ lies on the other side of the Sun, and has long been the predicted location of a hidden planet, since it could not be observed from Earth prior to the advent of satellite observation. Now, of course, we know there is no planet at that location.
