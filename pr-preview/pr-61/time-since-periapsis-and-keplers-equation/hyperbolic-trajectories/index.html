Hyperbolic Trajectories (e > 1)

In the section for ellipses, we found Eq. 216:

μ2h3t=∫0νdν(1+ecos⁡ν)2\frac{\mu^2}{h^3}t = \int_{0}^{\nu}\frac{d\nu}{\left(1 + e\cos\nu\right)^2}h3μ2​t=∫0ν​(1+ecosν)2dν​
(240)

The third solution to the right hand side, when e>1e > 1e>1 is:

∫dν(1+ecos⁡ν)2=1(e2−1)3/2[ee2−1sin⁡ν1+ecos⁡ν−ln⁡(e+1+e−1tan⁡ν2e+1−e−1tan⁡ν2)]\int\frac{d\nu}{\left(1 + e\cos \nu\right)^2} = \frac{1}{\left(e^2 - 1\right)^{3/2}}\left[\frac{e\sqrt{e^2 - 1}\sin \nu}{1 + e\cos \nu} - \ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{e + 1} + \sqrt{e - 1}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}}{\sqrt{e + 1} - \sqrt{e - 1}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)\right]∫(1+ecosν)2dν​=(e2−1)3/21​[1+ecosνee2−1​sinν​−ln(e+1​−e−1​tan2ν​e+1​+e−1​tan2ν​​)]
(241)

In Eq. 241, e>1e > 1e>1, so it will apply for hyperbolic trajectories.

For the hyperbola, combining Eq. 216 and Eq. 241 results in:

Mh=ee2−1sin⁡ν1+ecos⁡ν−ln⁡[e+1+e−1tan⁡ν2e+1−e−1tan⁡ν2]M_h = \frac{e\sqrt{e^2 - 1}\sin\nu}{1 + e\cos\nu} - \ln\left[\frac{\sqrt{e + 1} + \sqrt{e - 1}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}}{\sqrt{e + 1} - \sqrt{e - 1}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}}\right]Mh​=1+ecosνee2−1​sinν​−ln[e+1​−e−1​tan2ν​e+1​+e−1​tan2ν​​]
(242)

where

Mh=μ2h3t(e2−1)3/2M_h = \frac{\mu^2}{h^3} t \left(e^2 - 1\right)^{3/2}Mh​=h3μ2​t(e2−1)3/2
(243)

The hyperbolic mean anomaly, like the elliptical mean anomaly, is a monotonic function of the true anomaly, as shown in Fig. 61.

The ratio y/by/by/b is the definition of the hyperbolic sine of the angle FFF:

sinh⁡F=yb\sinh F = \frac{y}{b}sinhF=by​
(244)

Then, using the hyperbolic trigonometric identity:

cosh⁡2c−sinh⁡2c=1\cosh^2 c - \sinh^2 c = 1cosh2c−sinh2c=1
(245)

we also define

cosh⁡F=xa\cosh F = \frac{x}{a}coshF=ax​
(246)
Note

The hyperbolic angle FFF is weird. The reason we don’t draw it on the figure is because hyperbolic angles aren’t angles per se. Instead, they can be interpreted as half the area between the xxx-axis and a line drawn from the origin to the point of interest, bounded by the hyperbola. I think. At least, that’s my best interpretation from what I’ve been able to read. YMMV.

Another way of thinking about this is by analogy to a circle. For a circle, we can draw any two lines from the center of the circle to the perimeter. These two lines will have an angle ϕ\phiϕ between them, and the area between them will be:

Acircular sector=r2ϕ2A_{\text{circular sector}} = \frac{r^2 \phi}{2}Acircular sector​=2r2ϕ​
(247)

If the circle is a unit circle (r=1r = 1r=1), then the area of the sector will be equal to the angle divided by two. Turned around, the angle is equal to twice the area:

ϕ=2A12\phi = \frac{2A}{1^2}ϕ=122A​
(248)

Similarly, the hyperbolic angle is defined on the unit hyperbola as twice the area between two lines that start at the origin and touch the hyperbola, called a hyperbolic sector.

However, the circular angle between the xxx-axis and the ray from the origin is not the same as the hyperbolic angle.

You can read more about hyperbolic angles on Brilliant and on Wikipedia.

We can relate FFF to the true anomaly ν\nuν by plugging in y=rsin⁡νy = r\sin\nuy=rsinν, and the orbit equation for rrr. We also note that b=ae2−1b = a\sqrt{e^2 - 1}b=ae2−1​. Then:

F=sinh⁡−1(sin⁡νe2−11+ecos⁡ν)=ln⁡(sin⁡νe2−1+cos⁡ν+e1+ecos⁡ν)F = \sinh^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin\nu\sqrt{e^2 - 1}}{1 + e\cos\nu}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{\sin\nu\sqrt{e^2 - 1} + \cos\nu + e}{1 + e\cos\nu}\right)F=sinh−1(1+ecosνsinνe2−1​​)=ln(1+ecosνsinνe2−1​+cosν+e​)
(249)

After some more trigonometry and algebra, we find:

F=ln⁡[e+1+e−1tan⁡ν2e+1−e−1tan⁡ν2]F= \ln\left[\frac{\sqrt{e + 1} + \sqrt{e - 1}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}}{\sqrt{e + 1} - \sqrt{e - 1}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}}\right]F=ln[e+1​−e−1​tan2ν​e+1​+e−1​tan2ν​​]
(250)

And, with a little more algebra and trigonometry, we find an equation for FFF in terms of ν\nuν more directly, analogous to the ellipse:

tanh⁡F2=e−1e+1tan⁡ν2\tanh\frac{F}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{e - 1}{e + 1}}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}tanh2F​=e+1e−1​​tan2ν​
(251)

Kepler’s Equation for the Hyperbola¶

Substituting this back into Eq. 242, we find Kepler’s equation for the hyperbola:

Mh=esinh⁡F−FM_h = e\sinh F - FMh​=esinhF−F
(252)

As with the ellipse, Kepler’s equation can be solved easily if FFF is known. However, if time is the known quantity, then Kepler’s equation is transcendental and must be solved numerically. The form of the equation for the Newton solver is f(F)=0f(F) = 0f(F)=0, or:

f(F)=0=esinh⁡F−F−Mhf(F) = 0 = e\sinh F - F - M_hf(F)=0=esinhF−F−Mh​
(253)

To aid in the numerical solution, the derivative of Kepler’s equation for the hyperbola is:

f′(F)=ecosh⁡F−1f'(F) = e \cosh F - 1f′(F)=ecoshF−1
(254)

Note that the yyy-axis plots the log base 10 of MhM_hMh​. To use the graph, take the log base 10 of whatever value you calculate for MhM_hMh​ and find that on the graph. For example, assume that Mh=M_h =Mh​= 40.69 rad and e=e =e= 2.5. Then,

log⁡10(40.69)=1.6\log_{10}(40.69) = 1.6log10​(40.69)=1.6
