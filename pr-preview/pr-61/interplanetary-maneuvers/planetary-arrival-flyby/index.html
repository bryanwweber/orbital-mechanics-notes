Planetary Arrival: Flyby

As we discussed in the capture section, the options that a spacecraft has when arriving at a planet are to impact the planet, enter a capture orbit, or do a flyby to change its heliocentric velocity vector. In this section, we’ll consider the case of a flyby of the arrival planet.

Let’s begin with a few definitions. All capital letters will refer to heliocentric velocities, while lower case letters refer to geocentric velocities. Furthermore, the superscript sss will refer to the spacecraft, while variables with no superscript refer to the planet. Finally, subscript 1 refers to the arrival point at the sphere of influence and subscript 2 refers to the departure point from the sphere of influence.

The goal in this analysis is to determine the heliocentric velocity vector when the spacecraft exits the sphere of influence, 𝑽2s\vector{V}_2^sV2s​. This vector is then used to determine the orbital elements of the spacecraft’s modified heliocentric orbit.

To calculate the departure velocity vector, we will treat the arrival and departure events independently. We will also remove the assumption that v∞v_{\infty}v∞​ be parallel to the planet’s heliocentric velocity vector.

Arrival Trajectory¶

When the spacecraft arrives at the target planet, its trajectory will be a hyperbola relative to the planet. When crossing the sphere of influence of the planet, the spacecraft can be in one of two orientations:

  1. crossing in front of the planet, as shown in Fig. 92, called a leading-side flyby.

  2. crossing behind the planet, as shown in Fig. 93, called a trailing-side flyby

The angle δ\deltaδ, given by Eq. 161, is the turn angle. Note that δ\deltaδ is positive counterclockwise, so it is negative for the trailing-side flyby.

Arrival Heliocentric Velocity¶

To determine the spacecraft’s hyperbolic orbital elements, we need to find 𝒗∞,1\vector{v}_{\infty,1}v∞,1​, the excess velocity vector at arrival. The excess velocity vector can be found by taking the vector difference of the planet and spacecraft heliocentric velocity vectors:

𝒗∞,1=𝑽1s−𝑽\vector{v}_{\infty,1} = \vector{V}_1^s - \vector{V}v∞,1​=V1s​−V
(416)

where 𝑽\vector{V}V is the orbital velocity of the planet and 𝑽1s\vector{V}_1^sV1s​ is the spacecraft’s heliocentric velocity vector. This vector sum is shown visually in Fig. 92 and Fig. 93 in the inset figure.

In the heliocentric frame, the spacecraft’s velocity can be split into two components:

𝑽1s=V1,rs𝒖^r+V1,⊥s𝒖^⊥=μsunh1e1sin⁡ν1𝒖^r+μsunh1(1+e1cos⁡ν1)𝒖^⊥\vector{V}_1^s = V_{1,r}^s \uvec{u}_r + V_{1,\perp}^s \uvec{u}_{\perp} = \frac{\mu_{\text{sun}}}{h_1} e_1 \sin\nu_1 \uvec{u}_r + \frac{\mu_{\text{sun}}}{h_1}\left(1 + e_1 \cos\nu_1\right) \uvec{u}_{\perp}V1s​=V1,rs​u^r​+V1,⊥s​u^⊥​=h1​μsun​​e1​sinν1​u^r​+h1​μsun​​(1+e1​cosν1​)u^⊥​
(417)

where e1e_1e1​, h1h_1h1​, and ν1\nu_1ν1​ are the eccentricity, angular momentum, and true anomaly, respectively, of the heliocentric transfer orbit.

Geocentric Unit Vectors¶

To simplify the calculation of 𝒗∞\vector{v}_{\infty}v∞​, we would like to work with unit vectors attached to the planet, rather than the Sun. The 𝒖^r\uvec{u}_ru^r​-𝒖^⊥\uvec{u}_{\perp}u^⊥​ coordinate system, which is attached to the heliocentric orbit, can be converted into a geocentric system with the unit vectors 𝒖^V\uvec{u}_{V}u^V​ and 𝒖^S\uvec{u}_{S}u^S​.

𝒖^V\uvec{u}_Vu^V​ points along the planet’s orbital velocity vector. Since we assumed that the planet’s orbit is circular, the planet’s orbital velocity vector is always perpendicular to its radial vector. In other words:

𝒖^V=𝒖^⊥\uvec{u}_V = \uvec{u}_{\perp}u^V​=u^⊥​
(418)

The other geocentric unit vector corresponds to the radial direction, but is oriented in the opposite way. Thus:

𝒖^S=−𝒖^r\uvec{u}_S = -\uvec{u}_{r}u^S​=−u^r​
(419)

Thus, in the geocentric coordinate system, the planet’s orbital velocity is:

𝑽=V𝒖^V=μsunR𝒖^V\vector{V} = V \uvec{u}_V = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_{\text{sun}}}{R}} \uvec{u}_VV=Vu^V​=Rμsun​​​u^V​
(420)

where RRR is the planet’s orbital radius around the Sun. Similarly, the spacecraft’s heliocentric velocity can be transformed to the geocentric coordinate system:

𝑽1s=V1scos⁡α1𝒖^V+V1ssin⁡α1𝒖^S\vector{V}_1^s = V_1^s \cos\alpha_1 \uvec{u}_V + V_1^s \sin\alpha_1 \uvec{u}_SV1s​=V1s​cosα1​u^V​+V1s​sinα1​u^S​
(421)

where V1sV_1^sV1s​ is the magnitude of the spacecraft’s heliocentric velocity and α1\alpha_1α1​ is the angle that 𝑽1s\vector{V}_1^sV1s​ makes with 𝑽\vector{V}V. Thus, α1\alpha_1α1​ is the flight path angle of the spacecraft, and its value is found from Eq. 128:

α1=tan⁡−1V1,rsV1,⊥s=e1sin⁡ν11+e1cos⁡ν1\alpha_1 = \tan^{-1} \frac{V_{1,r}^s}{V_{1,\perp}^s} = \frac{e_1\sin\nu_1}{1 + e_1\cos\nu_1}α1​=tan−1V1,⊥s​V1,rs​​=1+e1​cosν1​e1​sinν1​​
(422)

Arrival Excess Velocity Vector¶

In Fig. 92 and Fig. 93, we can break the excess velocity vector into the geocentric coordinate system:

𝒗∞,1=(V1scos⁡α1−V)𝒖^V+V1ssin⁡α1𝒖^S\vector{v}_{\infty,1} = \left(V_1^s\cos\alpha_1 - V\right) \uvec{u}_V + V_1^s \sin\alpha_1 \uvec{u}_Sv∞,1​=(V1s​cosα1​−V)u^V​+V1s​sinα1​u^S​
(423)

Finally, we can compute the magnitude of the excess velocity:

v∞=(V1s)2+V2−2V1sVcos⁡α1v_{\infty} = \sqrt{\left(V_1^s\right)^2 + V^2 - 2 V_1^s V \cos\alpha_1}v∞​=(V1s​)2+V2−2V1s​Vcosα1​​
(424)

The magnitude V1sV_1^sV1s​ can be found from the velocity components in Eq. 417, and VVV is given by Eq. 420.

The magnitude of the excess velocity depends only on the semimajor axis of the hyperbola. Since the semimajor axis is constant, the magnitude of the excess velocity is also constant. Thus, we drop the subscript for arrival and departure for the magnitude.

Once we have the magnitude of the excess velocity, and when we specify a value for rpr_prp​, we can apply the techniques in the capture section to compute the orbital elements of the hyperbola.

Note

The techniques described in this section can also be used for a capture orbit if the excess velocity vector is not parallel to the planet’s orbital velocity.

Departure Trajectory¶

Now that we have 𝒗∞,1\vector{v}_{\infty,1}v∞,1​ from Eq. 423, we are ready to calculate the departure trajectory. The 𝒗∞\vector{v}_{\infty}v∞​ vector is rotated through the turn angle δ\deltaδ. We need a consistent reference line from which the angle of the 𝒗∞\vector{v}_{\infty}v∞​ vector can be measured.

The easiest choice for a reference line is the planet’s heliocentric velocity vector. As shown in Fig. 94, we can define an angle ϕ\phiϕ from the planet’s velocity vector 𝑽\vector{V}V to 𝒗∞\vector{v}_{\infty}v∞​. ϕ1\phi_1ϕ1​ is defined for the angle at arrival and ϕ2\phi_2ϕ2​ is the angle at departure.

The relationship between the ϕ\phiϕ angles is:

ϕ2=ϕ1+δ\phi_2 = \phi_1 + \deltaϕ2​=ϕ1​+δ
(425)

Note that the sign of δ\deltaδ is important. For a leading-side flyby δ\deltaδ is positive, while for a trailing-side flyby δ\deltaδ is negative.

In the geocentric coordinate system, the excess velocity vector has components given by Eq. 423. From a right triangle, the angle ϕ1\phi_1ϕ1​ is found by:

tan⁡ϕ1=V1ssin⁡α1V1scos⁡α1−V\tan\phi_1 = \frac{V_1^s\sin\alpha_1}{V_1^s\cos\alpha_1 - V}tanϕ1​=V1s​cosα1​−VV1s​sinα1​​
(426)

Since the magnitude of the excess velocity is constant, we can find the departure excess velocity vector using ϕ2\phi_2ϕ2​ and the magnitude:

𝒗∞,2=v∞cos⁡ϕ2𝒖^V+v∞sin⁡ϕ2𝒖^S\vector{v}_{\infty,2} = v_{\infty}\cos\phi_2 \uvec{u}_V + v_{\infty} \sin\phi_2 \uvec{u}_Sv∞,2​=v∞​cosϕ2​u^V​+v∞​sinϕ2​u^S​
(427)

Then, finally, the heliocentric velocity vector at departure is found by the vector sum of 𝑽\vector{V}V and 𝒗∞,2\vector{v}_{\infty,2}v∞,2​:

𝑽2s=𝑽+𝒗∞,2=V+v∞cos⁡ϕ2𝒖^V+v∞sin⁡ϕ2𝒖^S\vector{V}_{2}^s = \vector{V} + \vector{v}_{\infty,2} = V + v_{\infty} \cos\phi_2\uvec{u}_V + v_{\infty}\sin\phi_2 \uvec{u}_SV2s​=V+v∞,2​=V+v∞​cosϕ2​u^V​+v∞​sinϕ2​u^S​
(428)

From Eq. 418 and Eq. 419, we see that the perpendicular and radial components of the heliocentric velocity are:

V⊥,2s=V+v∞cos⁡ϕ2Vr,2s=−v∞sin⁡ϕ2\begin{aligned} V_{\perp,2}^s &= V + v_{\infty} \cos\phi_2 & V_{r,2}^s &= -v_{\infty}\sin\phi_2 \end{aligned}V⊥,2s​​=V+v∞​cosϕ2​​Vr,2s​​=−v∞​sinϕ2​​
(429)

Using these two velocity components and the known radial distance of the planet from the Sun, we can compute the orbital elements of the new heliocentric orbit. The orbital angular momentum is found by:

h2=RV⊥,2h_2 = R V_{\perp,2}h2​=RV⊥,2​
(430)

where RRR is the planet’s orbital radius. Using the orbit equation, Eq. 123 and Eq. 125, we can solve for the eccentricity and true anomaly:

R=h22μsun11+e2cos⁡ν2R = \frac{h_2^2}{\mu_{\text{sun}}} \frac{1}{1 + e_2 \cos\nu_2}R=μsun​h22​​1+e2​cosν2​1​
(431)

and

Vr,2s=μsunh2e2sin⁡ν2V_{r,2}^s = \frac{\mu_{\text{sun}}}{h_2}e_2\sin\nu_2Vr,2s​=h2​μsun​​e2​sinν2​
(432)

Example¶

A spacecraft will use a flyby maneuver around Venus to change its heliocentric orbit. The spacecraft departs the orbit of Neptune on a velocity perpendicular to a line to the Sun, and meets Venus at a true anomaly of 120° relative to the departure point. The periapsis altitude is 300 km. Calculate the spacecraft’s new heliocentric orbital elements after a leading-side flyby and a trailing-side flyby.

First, we need to calculate the arrival heliocentric velocity vector. The spacecraft departs Neptune’s orbit at aphelion of the transfer orbit. We know the departure point is aphelion because the velocity vector is perpendicular to the radius vector. We can find the orbital elements of the transfer orbit by using the orbit equation, Eq. 123 at the departure and arrival points.

RNeptune=h12μsun11−e1DepartureRVenus=h12μsun11−e1cos⁡ν1Arrival\begin{aligned} R_{\text{Neptune}} &= \frac{h_1^2}{\mu_{\text{sun}}} \frac{1}{1 - e_1} & \text{Departure} \\ R_{\text{Venus}} &= \frac{h_1^2}{\mu_{\text{sun}}} \frac{1}{1 - e_1\cos\nu_1} & \text{Arrival} \end{aligned}RNeptune​RVenus​​=μsun​h12​​1−e1​1​=μsun​h12​​1−e1​cosν1​1​​DepartureArrival​

where ν\nuν at Neptune departure is 180° and ν1=\nu_1 =ν1​= -60° or 300°. This pair of equations can be simultaneously solved for h1h_1h1​ and e1e_1e1​ to give the parameters of the transfer orbit. Solving first for e1e_1e1​, we find:

e1=RNeptune−RVenusRNeptune+RVenuscos⁡ν1e_1 = \frac{R_{\text{Neptune}} - R_{\text{Venus}}}{R_{\text{Neptune}} + R_{\text{Venus}}\cos\nu_1}e1​=RNeptune​+RVenus​cosν1​RNeptune​−RVenus​​

Then, h1h_1h1​ is found from the orbit equation at Neptune departure:

h1=μsunRNeptune(1−e1)h_1 = \sqrt{\mu_{\text{sun}} R_{\text{Neptune}}\left(1 - e_1\right)}h1​=μsun​RNeptune​(1−e1​)​
import numpy as np

mu_sun = 1.32712E11  # km**3/s**2
R_Neptune = 4.50489E9  # km
R_Venus = 1.08209E8  # km

nu_1 = np.radians(300)
e_1 = (R_Neptune - R_Venus) / (R_Neptune + R_Venus * np.cos(nu_1))
h_1 = np.sqrt(mu_sun * R_Neptune * (1 - e_1))

This gives an eccentricity of e1=e_1 =e1​= 0.9644 and h1=h_1 =h1​= 4.61E+09 km2/s. With e1e_1e1​, nu1nu_1nu1​, and h1h_1h1​, we can find the heliocentric velocity components at Venus using Eq. 124 and Eq. 125:

V_p1 = h_1 / R_Venus
V_r1 = mu_sun / h_1 * e_1 * np.sin(nu_1)

The heliocentric velocity components are V⊥,1s=V_{\perp,1}^s =V⊥,1s​= 42.64 km/s and Vr,1s=V_{r,1}^s =Vr,1s​= -24.02 km/s. Next, we need to find the excess velocity vector at arrival, and its magnitude:

V_1 = np.sqrt(V_p1**2 + V_r1**2)
alpha_1 = np.arctan2(V_r1, V_p1)

V_Venus = np.sqrt(mu_sun / R_Venus)

v_infty1_V = V_p1 - V_Venus
v_infty1_S = -V_r1
v_infty = np.sqrt(v_infty1_V**2 + v_infty1_S**2)

The spacecraft heliocentric velocity magnitude is V1s=V_1^s =V1s​= 48.94 km/s, the flight path angle is α1=\alpha_1 =α1​= -29.40°, and the velocity of Venus is VVenus=V_{\text{Venus}} =VVenus​= 35.02 km/s. The excess velocity vector is 𝒗∞,1=\vector{v}_{\infty,1} =v∞,1​= 7.62 𝒖^V\uvec{u}_Vu^V​ - 24.02 𝒖^S\uvec{u}_Su^S​ km/s, and its magnitude is v∞=v_{\infty} =v∞​= 25.20 km/s.

Now we can compute the geocentric orbital elements of the flyby trajectory. In particular, we need to calculate the turn angle δ\deltaδ using Eq. 161, which requires the eccentricity eee from Eq. 401. Then, we can calculate the ϕ\phiϕ angles.

r_p = 300 + 6051.8  # km

The eccentricity of the hyperbola is e=e =e= 13.4193 and the turn angle is δ=\delta =δ= 8.55°. This gives turn angles of ϕ1=\phi_1 =ϕ1​= 72.41° at arrival, ϕ2=\phi_2 =ϕ2​= 80.96° when the flyby occurs on the leading side, and ϕ2=\phi_2 =ϕ2​= 63.86° for the trailing-side flyby. Notice that δ\deltaδ is treated as negative for the trailing-side flyby!

Finally, we can compute the departure excess velocity vector and the departure heliocentric velocity vector, which allows us to compute the new heliocentric orbital elements.

Leading-Side Flyby¶

Let’s start with the leading-side flyby.

v_infty2_V = v_infty * np.cos(phi_2_leading)
v_infty2_S = v_infty * np.sin(phi_2_leading)

V_p2 = V_Venus + v_infty2_V
V_r2 = - v_infty2_S
V_2 = np.sqrt(V_p2**2 + V_r2**2)
Delta_V = abs(V_2 - V_1)

This gives a heliocentric velocity of V2s=V_2^s =V2s​= 46.25 km/s, with components V⊥,2s=V_{\perp,2}^s =V⊥,2s​= 38.98 km/s and Vr,2s=V_{r,2}^s =Vr,2s​= -24.89 km/s. This is a decrease of about 2.69 km/s in heliocentric speed, as expected for a leading-side flyby.

The departure angular momentum is found by Eq. 430. From Eq. 431, we find:

e2cos⁡ν2=h22μsunR−1e_2 \cos\nu_2 = \frac{h_2^2}{\mu_{\text{sun}} R} - 1e2​cosν2​=μsun​Rh22​​−1

Similarly, from Eq. 432, we find:

e2sin⁡ν2=Vr,2sh2μsune_2 \sin\nu_2 = \frac{V_{r,2}^s h_2}{\mu_{\text{sun}}}e2​sinν2​=μsun​Vr,2s​h2​​

Taking the ratio of Eq. 433 and Eq. 434, we find:

tan⁡ν2=RVr,2sh2h22−Rμsun\tan\nu_2 = \frac{R V_{r,2}^s h_2}{h_2^2 - R\mu_{\text{sun}}}tanν2​=h22​−Rμsun​RVr,2s​h2​​

With h2h_2h2​, e2e_2e2​, and ν2\nu_2ν2​, we can calculate the other orbital elements of interest.

h_2 = R_Venus * V_p2

The true anomaly is ν2=\nu_2 =ν2​= -73.19°, so the spacecraft is approaching perihelion.

Trailing-Side Flyby¶

Now let’s do the trailing-side flyby. The calculations are all the same as the leading-side, but we expect the spacecraft to increase its heliocentric speed.

v_infty2_V = v_infty * np.cos(phi_2_trailing)
v_infty2_S = v_infty * np.sin(phi_2_trailing)

V_p2 = V_Venus + v_infty2_V
V_r2 = - v_infty2_S
V_2 = np.sqrt(V_p2**2 + V_r2**2)
Delta_V = abs(V_2 - V_1)
h_2 = R_Venus * V_p2
nu_2 = np.arctan2(R_Venus * V_r2 * h_2, h_2**2 - R_Venus * mu_sun)
e_2 = (V_r2 * h_2) / (mu_sun * np.sin(nu_2))
a_2 = h_2**2 / mu_sun / (e**2 - 1)

R_a2 = 2 * a_2 - R_p2
nu_infty_2 = np.arccos(-1 / e_2)

This gives a heliocentric velocity of V2s=V_2^s =V2s​= 51.37 km/s, with components V⊥,2s=V_{\perp,2}^s =V⊥,2s​= 46.12 km/s and Vr,2s=V_{r,2}^s =Vr,2s​= -22.63 km/s. This is an increase of about 2.43 km/s in heliocentric speed, as expected for a trailing-side flyby.

For the trailing-side flyby the eccentricity is e2=e_2 =e2​= 1.1240. Since e2>1e_2 > 1e2​>1, the new heliocentric trajectory is a hyperbola relative to the sun and the true anomaly of the asymptote is ν∞=\nu_{\infty} =ν∞​= 152.83°. This means the spacecraft is now on a trajectory to escape the solar system!

The current true anomaly is ν2=\nu_2 =ν2​= -49.20°, so the spacecraft is approaching perihelion, which will be at a distance of Rp,2=R_{p,2} =Rp,2​= 130006 km. Unfortunately, this is inside the equatorial radius of the Sun as shown in Table 9, so the spacecraft most likely would not survive the encounter.
