The Orbit Equation

Now, we’ll return to the equation of relative motion, Eq. 34, repeated here for reference:

𝒓¨=−(μr3)𝒓\ddot{\vector{r}} = -\left(\frac{\mu}{r^3}\right)\vector{r}r¨=−(r3μ​)r
(114)

Our goal is to be able to integrate this equation to find a scalar equation. An analytical equation will be more accurate than the numerical techniques we used earlier, and a scalar equation is easier to work with than a vector one.

We start by taking the cross product of this equation with the angular momentum, 𝒉\vector{h}h:

𝒓¨×𝒉=−(μr3)𝒓×𝒉\ddot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h} = -\left(\frac{\mu}{r^3}\right)\vector{r}\cross\vector{h}r¨×h=−(r3μ​)r×h
(115)

To make this easy to integrate, we want both sides to be d/dt(…)d/dt(\ldots)d/dt(…). Let’s try to replace the left-hand side of the equation. Since 𝒓¨=d/dt(𝒓˙)\ddot{\vector{r}} = d/dt\left(\dot{\vector{r}}\right)r¨=d/dt(r˙), let’s try to pull a d/dtd/dtd/dt out. By the product rule of differentiation, we find:

ddt(𝒓˙×𝒉)=𝒓¨×𝒉+𝒓˙×𝒉˙\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h}\right) = \ddot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h} + \dot{\vector{r}}\cross\dot{\vector{h}}dtd​(r˙×h)=r¨×h+r˙×h˙
(116)

But the angular momentum is constant, so its derivative 𝒉˙=𝟎\dot{\vector{h}} = \vector{0}h˙=0 and the second term in Eq. 116 is zero. Therefore:

ddt(𝒓˙×𝒉)=𝒓¨×𝒉=−(μr3)𝒓×𝒉\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h}\right) = \ddot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h} = -\left(\frac{\mu}{r^3}\right)\vector{r}\cross\vector{h}dtd​(r˙×h)=r¨×h=−(r3μ​)r×h
(117)

So, the left-hand side of the formula is in terms of d/dt(…)d/dt(\ldots)d/dt(…). Let’s get the right-hand side into the same form so we can simplify. After a bunch of algebra, we find:

−μr3𝒓×𝒉=μddt(𝒓r)-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\vector{r}\cross\vector{h} = \mu\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\vector{r}}{r}\right)−r3μ​r×h=μdtd​(rr​)
(118)

Substituting everything together, we find:

ddt(𝒓˙×𝒉)=ddt(μ𝒓r)\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h}\right) = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\mu\frac{\vector{r}}{r}\right)dtd​(r˙×h)=dtd​(μrr​)
(119)

Rearranging:

ddt(𝒓˙×𝒉−μ𝒓r)=𝟎\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h} - \mu\frac{\vector{r}}{r}\right) = \vector{0}dtd​(r˙×h−μrr​)=0
(120)

This equation can be integrated to find:

𝒓˙×𝒉−μ𝒓r=𝑩\dot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h} - \mu\frac{\vector{r}}{r} = \vector{B}r˙×h−μrr​=B
(121)

where 𝑩\vector{B}B is called the Laplace vector and is the constant of integration. The Laplace vector has the same dimensions as μ\muμ, so we can transform it into a dimensionless number by dividing the equation by μ\muμ:

𝒓r+𝒆=𝒓˙×𝒉μ\frac{\vector{r}}{r} + \vector{e} = \frac{\dot{\vector{r}}\cross\vector{h}}{\mu}rr​+e=μr˙×h​
(122)

where 𝒆=𝑩/μ\vector{e} = \vector{B}/\mue=B/μ and is called the eccentricity vector. Since 𝑩\vector{B}B lies in the orbital plane, 𝒆\vector{e}e also lies in the orbital plane. The line along 𝒆\vector{e}e is called the apse line. These coordinates are shown in Fig. 30.

We now want to transform Eq. 122 to be in terms of rrr and ν\nuν, which is called the true anomaly, defined as the angle from the apse line to the m2m_2m2​. This will result in a scalar equation, which is easier to work with than the vector equation.

To obtain a scalar equation, we take the dot product of Eq. 122 with 𝒓\vector{r}r. After some algebra, we end up at:

r=h2μ11+ecos⁡νr = \frac{h^2}{\mu}\frac{1}{1 + e\cos\nu}r=μh2​1+ecosν1​
(123)

where e=∥𝒆∥e = \mag{\vector{e}}e=∥e∥ is called the eccentricity. Eq. 123 is called the orbit equation and it defines the path of m2m_2m2​ around m1m_1m1​, relative to m1m_1m1​. In this equation, hhh, eee, and μ\muμ are all constant. Since eee is the magnitude of 𝒆\vector{e}e, it is strictly positive, e≥0e \geq 0e≥0.

Important

Put a big star next to Eq. 123. We are going to use it for the rest of the course!

Conic sections are the curve formed by the intersection of a plane and a cone. 3Blue1Brown has an awesome video covering why ellipses are formed from the intersection of a cone and a plane.

The orbit equation describes conic sections, meaning that all orbits are one of four types, as shown in Fig. 31. The particular type of orbit is determined by the magnitude of the eccentricity:

  1. Circles: e=0e = 0e=0

  2. Ellipses: 0<e<10 < e < 10<e<1

  3. Parabolas: e=1e = 1e=1

  4. Hyperbolas e>1e > 1e>1

We are going to handle each of these in turn in a few sections. In the meantime, we are going to do a little bit of work directly with the orbit equation.
