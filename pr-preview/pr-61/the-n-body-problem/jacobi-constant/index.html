The Jacobi Constant

Let’s return now to the graph of the potential function, Fig. 17. The potential function represents the potential energy that the tertiary mass will have if it is located at a given x∗x^*x∗-y∗y^*y∗ point in the orbital plane. The tertiary mass will also have some speed, v∗v^*v∗, with a corresponding kinetic energy.

From the law of conservation of energy, we know that the sum of the kinetic and potential energies of the tertiary mass will be constant. We will use the fact that energy is constant to determine what areas of space are accessible for the tertiary mass with a certain initial condition.

Potential Energy¶

The potential energy is given in Eq. 88 and repeated here:

U(x∗,y∗)=−1−π2σ−π2ψ−12[(1−π2)σ2+π2ψ2]U(x^*, y^*) = -\frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma} - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi} - \frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1 - \pi_2\right)\sigma^2 + \pi_2 \psi^2\right]U(x∗,y∗)=−σ1−π2​​−ψπ2​​−21​[(1−π2​)σ2+π2​ψ2]

This equation has three terms:

  1. The gravitational potential energy due to the position of mmm relative to m2m_2m2​

  2. The gravitational potential energy due to the position of mmm relative to m1m_1m1​

  3. The potential energy due to the centrifugal force induced by the rotation of the reference frame.

Kinetic Energy¶

The mass-specific kinetic energy is:

12(v∗)2=12[(x˙∗)2+(y˙∗)2]\frac{1}{2} \left(v^*\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\dot{x}^*\right)^2 + \left(\dot{y}^*\right)^2\right]21​(v∗)2=21​[(x˙∗)2+(y˙​∗)2]
(90)

Combining Eq. 88 and Eq. 90, we find from conservation of energy:

12(v∗)2−1−π2σ−π2ψ−12[(1−π2)σ2+π2ψ2]=J\frac{1}{2} \left(v^*\right)^2 - \frac{1 - \pi_2}{\sigma} - \frac{\pi_2}{\psi} - \frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1 - \pi_2\right)\sigma^2 + \pi_2 \psi^2\right] = J21​(v∗)2−σ1−π2​​−ψπ2​​−21​[(1−π2​)σ2+π2​ψ2]=J
(91)

The constant JJJ is called the Jacobi Constant, and represents the total energy of the tertiary mass relative to the rotating reference frame.

Interpreting the Jacobi Constant¶

Since JJJ is a constant, the total energy of the tertiary mass is fixed. The tertiary mass can trade kinetic energy for potential energy, and vice versa, but the total energy cannot change. This leads to two conclusions.

Consider the tertiary mass at some location (x1∗,y1∗)\left(x^*_1, y^*_1\right)(x1∗​,y1∗​) such that it has potential energy U1U_1U1​. Also assume that the velocity is zero, v1∗=v^*_1 =v1∗​= 0. Then, the Jacobi constant is just equal to U1U_1U1​, and the mass cannot “climb” any higher out of the potential energy surface. Thus, there is a region of space where the mass cannot access because it doesn’t have enough energy!

Remember, this is the velocity relative to the rotating coordinate system, not the inertial velocity.

Assume now that the velocity of the mass is v2∗v^*_2v2∗​ and the position is (x2∗,y2∗)\left(x^*_2, y^*_2\right)(x2∗​,y2∗​) so that the potential energy is U2U_2U2​. Then the Jacobi constant is equal to the sum of the kinetic and potential energies. If the mass wants to climb up the potential energy surface, it can trade kinetic energy for potential energy. Eventually, however, the kinetic energy and the velocity will go to zero, and the mass cannot climb any higher!

Forbidden Regions¶

Now, let’s turn this problem around. We want to know, for a given value of JJJ, what regions of space will be inaccessible. Consider the tertiary mass with a certain value of JJJ and at a particular location. Since JJJ and the position are known, the velocity can be calculated from Eq. 91.

Koon et al. (2011) also refer to the area where a particle cannot enter as the Hill’s region.

As the mass moves, it exchanges energy between kinetic energy (velocity) and potential energy (position). At some position, the JJJ will be equal to UUU, and the velocity will be (by definition) zero. Thus, the mass cannot travel any further in that direction.

For a given value of the Jacobi constant, we can calculate the contours of zero velocity positions by setting v∗=v^* =v∗= 0 in Eq. 91:

2(1−π2)σ+2π2ψ+[(1−π2)σ2+π2ψ2]+2J=0\frac{2 \left(1 - \pi_2\right)}{\sigma} + \frac{2 \pi_2}{\psi} + \left[\left(1 - \pi_2\right)\sigma^2 + \pi_2 \psi^2\right] + 2J = 0σ2(1−π2​)​+ψ2π2​​+[(1−π2​)σ2+π2​ψ2]+2J=0
(92)

Since the first three terms on the left are all positive, zero velocity curves correspond to negative values of the Jacobi constant.

At a given instant, assume that the position and velocity of the satellite are known, such that JJJ can be calculated. Fig. 22 tells us what regions of space a satellite can access purely by exchanging kinetic and potential energy.

One example of a use for these data is to determine whether or not a spacecraft can complete a free return trajectory from the moon back to Earth. A free return trajectory requires no additional propulsion. Depending on the exact trajectory, the return can result in the spacecraft entering Earth’s atmosphere for a safe landing, as was done in the Apollo 13 mission.

Imagine a spacecraft in orbit around the moon. The spacecraft has J=J =J= -1.797, corresponding to the upper left axis in Fig. 22. With this value of JJJ, the spacecraft cannot leave orbit around the moon, because it does not have enough energy to enter any of the gray areas.

Now, imagine the same spacecraft has J=J =J= -1.592. If the spacecraft is at the same position as before, its speed will have increased. This gives the spacecraft access to more of the space between the Earth and the moon. Now, if the spacecraft has the right initial position and speed for it, there is a possible trajectory that connects the moon and the Earth.

As JJJ increases, more and more of the space between the Earth and the moon becomes accessible to the spacecraft. Although the Jacobi constant cannot tell us what initial conditions are required for a given trajectory, it can tell us whether or not a given set of initial conditions will be able to get us where we want to go.
