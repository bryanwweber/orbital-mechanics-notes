Energy Is Conserved In Orbital Motion

The two masses m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​ in the two-body problem form a system, so they must follow all of the conservation laws:

  1. Conservation of mass

  2. Conservation of energy

  3. Conservation of momentum

Mass is trivially conserved in this system, since neither body is changing mass. In this section, we will prove that the two-body equation of motion satisfies conservation of energy.

In the rest of this section, we will assume the coordinate system is the co-moving system attached to m1m_1m1​, so we are working with the two-body problem in relative motion.

Takeaway Message

There are two important results in this section:

  1. The two-body equation of motion does satisfy conservation of energy, that is, total energy is constant

  2. The vis viva equation gives the proportions of kinetic and potential energy that make up the total energy at any location on the orbit:

E=v22−μrE = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r}E=2v2​−rμ​
(95)

An Intuitive Understanding of Energy¶

In the context of orbital mechanics, we care about two forms of energy:

  1. Kinetic energy, KE\KEKE

  2. Potential energy, V\PEV

These forms of mechanical energy are the only forms in this system, so the total energy must satisfy the law of conservation of energy:

KE+V=E\KE + \PE = EKE+V=E
(96)

where EEE is the total mechanical energy of the system and must be a constant.

Conservative forces allow infinite conversion of energy between forms. Non-conservative forces, such as friction and drag, result in a conversion of mechanical energy into heat.

The potential energy is due to the location of m2m_2m2​ in the gravitational field of m1m_1m1​, while the kinetic energy is due to the velocity of m2m_2m2​ relative to m1m_1m1​. Gravity is a conservative force, which means that any change of kinetic energy is associated with an equal and opposite change of potential energy.

As kinetic energy increases and the speed of m2m_2m2​ increases, the potential energy must decrease and m2m_2m2​ gets closer to m1m_1m1​. The point of highest speed is at the point of closest approach. Likewise, the point of lowest speed is at the farthest point on the orbit. You can examine the results from Two-Body Problem in the Co-Moving Frame to verify this is correct.

Now, let’s define each of the forms of mechanical energy a little more rigorously.

Conservation of Energy¶

Since the total mechanical energy, EEE, is a constant, its derivative with respect to time must be equal to zero:

dEdt=0=dKEdt+dVdt\frac{dE}{dt} = 0 = \frac{d\KE}{dt} + \frac{d\PE}{dt}dtdE​=0=dtdKE​+dtdV​
(97)

V=−Gm1m2r=−Gm1m2x2+y2+z2\PE = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r} = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}V=−rGm1​m2​​=−x2+y2+z2​Gm1​m2​​

In Eq. 98, the coordinates xxx, yyy, and zzz are functions of time. This mildly complicates taking the derivative dV/dtd\PE/dtdV/dt:

dVdt=−Gm1m2(x2+y2+z2)3/2(−xvx−yvy−zvz)\frac{d\PE}{dt} = -\frac{G m_1 m_2}{\left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}} \left(-x v_x - y v_y - z v_z\right)dtdV​=−(x2+y2+z2)3/2Gm1​m2​​(−xvx​−yvy​−zvz​)

where vxv_xvx​, vyv_yvy​, and vzv_zvz​ are the components of velocity in those directions. Substituting the definition of rrr and μ\muμ, we find:

dVdt=m2μr3(xvx+yvy+zvz)\frac{d\PE}{dt} = m_2 \frac{\mu}{r^3}\left(x v_x + y v_y + z v_z\right)dtdV​=m2​r3μ​(xvx​+yvy​+zvz​)

Kinetic Energy¶

The kinetic energy of m2m_2m2​ relative to m1m_1m1​ is:

KE=12m2v2=12m2(vx2+vy2+vz2)\KE = \frac{1}{2} m_2 v^2 = \frac{1}{2} m_2 \left(v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2\right)KE=21​m2​v2=21​m2​(vx2​+vy2​+vz2​)

where v=∥v∥v = \mag{\vector{v}}v=∥v∥ is the magnitude of the velocity. As with the potential energy, the components of the velocity are functions of time. Thus, the derivative of the kinetic energy with respect to time is:

where axa_xax​, aya_yay​, and aza_zaz​ are the relative acceleration components.

Application to Two-Body Relative Motion¶

In this section, we are going to prove that the two-body equation of motion, Eq. 34, satisfies conservation of energy. Eq. 34 is a vector equation, while energy is a scalar quantity. This means that energy is represented by a single number, it has a magnitude but no direction.

This is why the dot product is also called the scalar product.

To convert between vectors and scalars mathematically, we have to use the vector dot product. We are going to take the dot product of Eq. 34 with something, and now we have to decide what that something is.

As m2m_2m2​ orbits around m1m_1m1​, the conversion between kinetic and potential energy is done by work. The rate at which work is done is the power, and is in turn equal to the rate of change of the energy, dE/dtdE/dtdE/dt. We already saw dE/dtdE/dtdE/dt in Eq. 97, so that is probably a good place to start.

Finally, the power is calculated by taking the dot product of the force and velocity vectors. So, we will take the dot product of the velocity vector and Eq. 34.

r¨⋅v+μr3r⋅v=0(vxax+vyay+vzaz)+μr3(xvx+yvy+zvz)=0\begin{aligned} \ddot{\vector{r}} \cdot \vector{v} + \frac{\mu}{r^3} \vector{r} \cdot \vector{v} &= 0 \\ \left(v_x a_x + v_y a_y + v_z a_z\right) + \frac{\mu}{r^3} \left(x v_x + y v_y + z v_z\right) &= 0 \end{aligned}r¨⋅v+r3μ​r⋅v(vx​ax​+vy​ay​+vz​az​)+r3μ​(xvx​+yvy​+zvz​)​=0=0​
(103)

Now, if we multiply through by m2m_2m2​ (which doesn’t change anything), we see two things:

  1. The first term on the left is identical to dKE/dtd\KE/dtdKE/dt, Eq. 102

  2. The second term on the left is identical to dV/dtd\PE/dtdV/dt, Eq. 100

Therefore, Eq. 103 shows that the two-body equation of motion can be converted into power, and the sum of the power is zero. This precisely satisfies the condition in Eq. 97!

Note

I’m not sure I’ve given this result enough oomph. What we have here is a proof that the two-body equation of motion satisfies the law of conservation of energy!

The vis viva Equation¶

Now that we have proven that the two-body equation of motion satisfies conservation of energy, we can use the conservation law to derive another useful equation. Starting from Eq. 97, let’s integrate with respect to time:

∫0dt=∫ddt(v22)dt−∫ddt(μr)dtE=v22+(C−μr)\begin{aligned} \int 0 dt &= \int \frac{d}{dt} \left(\frac{v^2}{2}\right) dt - \int \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\mu}{r}\right) dt \\ E &= \frac{v^2}{2} + \left(C - \frac{\mu}{r}\right) \end{aligned}∫0dtE​=∫dtd​(2v2​)dt−∫dtd​(rμ​)dt=2v2​+(C−rμ​)​
(104)

where CCC is an arbitrary constant of integration. By choosing a value for CCC, we are setting the reference point for the potential energy. For instance, we could choose the reference point at r=REr = R_Er=RE​, the radius of the Earth, which would give some finite value for CCC. However, it is most convenient to choose C=0C=0C=0, corresponding to a reference point at infinite radius.

Eq. 104 is a statement of the conservation of energy of the system. It is called the vis viva equation, which translates as the living force equation due to its central role in understanding how objects orbit each other.
