Circular and Elliptical Orbits (e < 1)

As we discussed in the last section, we need to find equations for the mean anomaly and eccentric anomaly. In this section we’ll find three equations that we need:

  1. An equation relating the mean anomaly to the time since periapsis:

    Me=2πtT=μ2h3t(1−e2)3/2M_e = \frac{2\pi t}{T} = \frac{\mu^2}{h^3} t \left(1 - e^2\right)^{3/2}Me​=T2πt​=h3μ2​t(1−e2)3/2
    (195)

  2. An equation relating the eccentric anomaly to the true anomaly

    tan⁡E2=1−e1+etan⁡ν2\tan\frac{E}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}tan2E​=1+e1−e​​tan2ν​
    (196)

  3. An equation relating the mean anomaly to the eccentric anomaly

    Me=E−esin⁡EM_e = E - e \sin{E}Me​=E−esinE
    (197)

With these three equations, we can solve either of the cases of knowing the time since periapsis; or knowing the true anomaly.

For the circle and ellipse, we will follow two methods to derive the appropriate equations:

  1. Using a geometric argument to relate swept areas on the orbit and a circumscribing circle

  2. Using calculus and trigonometric transformations starting from the orbit equation

These methods result in the same result, but some people may find one easier to follow than the other.

Swept Area Inside the Orbit¶

Since Kepler’s second law says that equal areas are swept in equal times, the swept area is directly proportional to the elapsed time. Let’s define a variable to capture this idea and call it the Mean Area, AmA_mAm​:

Am=CtA_m = C tAm​=Ct
(198)

where CCC is the constant of proportionality. For an elliptical orbit, the swept area is the elapsed fraction of the orbital period (t/Tt/Tt/T) multiplied by the total area of the ellipse (πab\pi a bπab):

We’ll use the example of an ellipse here, but the procedure shown here is similar for a parabola or hyperbola. They just end up with different values for CCC.

Am=Ct=πabtTA_m = C t = \pi a b \frac{t}{T}Am​=Ct=πabTt​
(199)

where aaa and bbb are the semimajor and semiminor axes, and TTT is the orbital period.

This area is shown in Fig. 52 as m1m_1m1​-PPP-BBB.

Swept Area Inside a Circumscribing Circle¶

The next step is to find another equation for AmA_mAm​ so that we can solve for ttt in Eq. 199. We already used Kepler’s second law to define Eq. 199, so continuing to work with the ellipse will be kind of a pain.

The circle here is called a circumscribing circle because it is tangent to the ellipse at two points, on both ends of the major axis. These positions are also the periapsis and apoapsis.

Instead, we can work with a circle, where it’s much easier to calculate areas. We are going to transform the ellipse into a circle by stretching it in the vertical direction, ensuring that the circle and ellipse continue to touch at the point PPP. While the ellipse is stretching, the swept area is also stretching. This process is animated in Fig. 53.

Notice that the original area m1m_1m1​-PPP-BBB has been transformed to the area m1m_1m1​-PPP-QQQ. Our goal now is to find the new area, then scale that area back down to the right size for Eq. 199. To find the area m1m_1m1​-PPP-QQQ, we make two observations:

  1. The region OOO-PPP-QQQ is a circular sector

  2. The region OOO-m1m_1m1​-QQQ is a triangle

These regions are shown on Fig. 54.

Using these regions, we can find the area m1m_1m1​-PPP-QQQ:

m1-P-Q=O-P-Q−O-m1-Q=a2E2−a2esin⁡E2m_1\text{-}P\text{-}Q = O\text{-}P\text{-}Q - O\text{-}m_1\text{-}Q = \frac{a^2 E}{2} - \frac{a^2 e \sin{E}}{2}m1​-P-Q=O-P-Q−O-m1​-Q=2a2E​−2a2esinE​
(200)

where aaa is the semimajor axis of the ellipse and the radius of the circle, EEE is the included angle in the circular sector, and eee is the eccentricity of the ellipse.

Relating the Two Areas Together¶

The final step is to scale the area m1m_1m1​-PPP-QQQ back down to the right size and then equate it with AmA_mAm​. The scaling factor for m1m_1m1​-PPP-QQQ is the ratio of the semi-minor axis, bbb, to the semi-major axis, aaa:

Am=ba(m1-P-Q)=abE2−abesin⁡E2=12ab(E−esin⁡E)A_m = \frac{b}{a} \left(m_1\text{-}P\text{-}Q\right) = \frac{abE}{2} - \frac{abe \sin{E}}{2} = \frac{1}{2} a b \left(E - e \sin{E}\right)Am​=ab​(m1​-P-Q)=2abE​−2abesinE​=21​ab(E−esinE)
(201)

where we have substituted the result of Eq. 200 and simplified. Finally, substituting Eq. 199:

πabtT=12ab(E−esin⁡E)→2πtT=E−esin⁡E\pi a b \frac{t}{T} = \frac{1}{2}a b \left(E - e \sin{E}\right) \rightarrow 2\pi\frac{t}{T} = E - e \sin{E}πabTt​=21​ab(E−esinE)→2πTt​=E−esinE
(202)

In Eq. 202, we can define the left side of the equation as the mean anomaly:

Me=2πtT=μ2h3t(1−e2)3/2M_e = 2\pi\frac{t}{T} = \frac{\mu^2}{h^3} t \left(1 - e^2\right)^{3/2}Me​=2πTt​=h3μ2​t(1−e2)3/2
(203)

where TTT is the orbital period, μ\muμ is the specific gravitational constant, hhh is the specific angular momentum, and eee is the eccentricity of the orbit. The last equality comes from the definition of the orbital period, Eq. 148.

Substituting Eq. 203 into Eq. 202, we find Kepler’s Equation for an ellipse:

Me=E−esin⁡EM_e = E - e \sin{E}Me​=E−esinE
(204)
Note

This equation is super important in the field of orbital mechanics, which is why it’s named for Kepler. Make sure to remember this equation!

Now we have two of the three equations we need to solve problems relating elapsed time and true anomaly:

  1. Eq. 203 gives us a relationship between ttt and MeM_eMe​

  2. Kepler’s equation, Eq. 204 gives us the relationship between MeM_eMe​ and EEE

The last step is to relate the eccentric anomaly to the true anomaly.

Eccentric Anomaly¶

The eccentric anomaly, EEE, is defined as the angle from the xxx axis to a point on a circle that circumscribes the orbital ellipse and where the point is located vertically above a point with true anomaly ν\nuν on the ellipse. This is shown in Fig. 51 and Fig. 55.

From Fig. 55, we see that the distance OPOPOP is equal to:

OP=ae+FP=acos⁡EOP = ae + FP = a \cos EOP=ae+FP=acosE
(205)

where aaa is the semimajor axis of the ellipse, eee is the eccentricity, and the distance FPFPFP is related to the true anomaly:

FP=rcos⁡νFP = r \cos\nuFP=rcosν
(206)

Combining Eq. 205 and Eq. 206, we find:

acos⁡E=ae+rcos⁡νa\cos E = ae + r\cos\nuacosE=ae+rcosν
(207)

Replacing rrr with the orbit equation in terms of the semimajor axis and simplifying, we find:

cos⁡E=e+cos⁡ν1+ecos⁡ν\cos E = \frac{e + \cos\nu}{1 + e\cos\nu}cosE=1+ecosνe+cosν​
(208)

or, solving for ν\nuν:

cos⁡ν=e−cos⁡Eecos⁡E−1\cos\nu = \frac{e - \cos E}{e\cos E - 1}cosν=ecosE−1e−cosE​
(209)

Unfortunately, these equations result in a quadrant ambiguity. We can resolve this by some further trigonometric transformations, which result in the equation:

tan⁡E2=1−e1+etan⁡ν2\tan\frac{E}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}tan2E​=1+e1−e​​tan2ν​
(210)

The inverse tangent function is not multi-valued for a given value of ν\nuν or EEE, so this resolves the quadrant ambiguity.

Note

Note: Eq. 210 can be solved in Python with np.arctan() and in Matlab with atan(). It is not necessary to use np.arctan2() or atan2(). Make sure all your arguments are in terms of radians!

Now with Eq. 204, Eq. 210, and Eq. 203 we have the three equations we need to solve time since periapsis problems!

An Alternative Derivation¶

Now we will pursue the second way to derive Kepler’s equation and the related equations for mean and eccentric anomaly. Recall the orbit equation, Eq. 123, defined in terms of the true anomaly:

r=h2μ11+ecos⁡νr = \frac{h^2}{\mu} \frac{1}{1 + e\cos\nu}r=μh2​1+ecosν1​
(211)

We now want to relate the true anomaly, ν\nuν, to time. The rate of change of the true anomaly, ν˙\dot{\nu}ν˙ is equal to the angular velocity of the position vector. This is exactly the azimuthal, also called the perpendicular, component of the velocity:

v⊥=rν˙=rdνdtv_{\perp} = r \dot{\nu} = r \frac{d\nu}{dt}v⊥​=rν˙=rdtdν​
(212)

The v⊥v_{\perp}v⊥​ term in Eq. 212 makes the equation more complicated than it needs to be, so we’d like to replace it. A more convenient form of Eq. 212 is found by using the specific angular momentum to replace v⊥v_{\perp}v⊥​, since hhh is constant:

h=rv⊥=r2ν˙⇒dνdt=hr2h = r v_{\perp} = r^2\dot{\nu} \Rightarrow \frac{d\nu}{dt} = \frac{h}{r^2}h=rv⊥​=r2ν˙⇒dtdν​=r2h​
(213)

Substituting the orbit equation to eliminate rrr and separating variables, we find:

μ2h3dt=dν(1+ecos⁡ν)2\frac{\mu^2}{h^3}dt = \frac{d\nu}{\left(1 + e\cos\nu\right)^2}h3μ2​dt=(1+ecosν)2dν​
(214)

Since μ\muμ and hhh are constant, the left side can be directly integrated:

μ2h3∫tptdt=∫0νdν(1+ecos⁡ν)2\frac{\mu^2}{h^3}\int_{t_p}^{t} dt = \int_{0}^{\nu}\frac{d\nu}{\left(1 + e\cos\nu\right)^2}h3μ2​∫tp​t​dt=∫0ν​(1+ecosν)2dν​
(215)

where tpt_ptp​ is defined as the time since periapsis. Remember that periapsis is when ν=0\nu = 0ν=0 by convention. Typically we will set tp=0t_p = 0tp​=0, such that:

μ2h3t=∫0νdν(1+ecos⁡ν)2\frac{\mu^2}{h^3}t = \int_{0}^{\nu}\frac{d\nu}{\left(1 + e\cos\nu\right)^2}h3μ2​t=∫0ν​(1+ecosν)2dν​
(216)

The integral on the right-hand side of Eq. 216 can be found in standard tables of integrals by Gradshteı̆n et al. (2007) and Zwillinger (2003). There are three forms of the equation, depending on the value of eee. In this section, we’ll show the equation for e<1e < 1e<1 and the other equations will be shown in the sections for the parabola and hyperbola.

In Gradshteı̆n et al. (2007) (available Table of Integrals, Series, and Products), the appropriate integrals are found on pages 172 and 173, No. 2.554-3 and related integrals for Eq. 217 and Eq. 241. In Zwillinger (2003) (available CRC Standard Mathematical Tables and Formulae), the appropriate integrals are found on pages 433 and 434, No. 354 and 324 for Eq. 217 and Eq. 241. I couldn’t find the form for Eq. 234 in those references. Note that a=1a = 1a=1 and b=eb = eb=e to relate the reference equations to Eq. 216.

∫dν(1+ecos⁡ν)2=1(1−e2)3/2[2tan⁡−1(1−e1+etan⁡ν2)−e1−e2sin⁡ν1+ecos⁡ν]\int\frac{d\nu}{\left(1 + e\cos \nu\right)^2} = \frac{1}{\left(1 - e^2\right)^{3/2}}\left[2\tan^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right)-\frac{e\sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin \nu}{1 + e \cos \nu}\right]∫(1+ecosν)2dν​=(1−e2)3/21​[2tan−1(1+e1−e​​tan2ν​)−1+ecosνe1−e2​sinν​]
(217)

In Eq. 217, e<1e < 1e<1, so it will apply for circular and elliptical orbits.

When Eq. 216 is combined with Eq. 217 we have a relationship where time is related to the true anomaly.

For circular and elliptical orbits, combining Eq. 216 and Eq. 217 results in:

μ2h3t=1(1−e2)3/2[2tan⁡−1(1−e1+etan⁡ν2)−e1−e2sin⁡ν1+ecos⁡ν]\frac{\mu^2}{h^3}t = \frac{1}{\left(1 - e^2\right)^{3/2}}\left[2\tan^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right)-\frac{e\sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin \nu}{1 + e \cos \nu}\right]h3μ2​t=(1−e2)3/21​[2tan−1(1+e1−e​​tan2ν​)−1+ecosνe1−e2​sinν​]
(218)

Mean Anomaly¶

We define the term in the square brackets in Eq. 218 to be the mean anomaly, MeM_eMe​, where the subscript eee indicates that this is for the ellipse. We will have different equations for the parabola and hyperbola.

Me=[2tan⁡−1(1−e1+etan⁡ν2)−e1−e2sin⁡ν1+ecos⁡ν]M_e = \left[2\tan^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}}\tan\frac{\nu}{2}\right)-\frac{e\sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin \nu}{1 + e \cos \nu}\right]Me​=[2tan−1(1+e1−e​​tan2ν​)−1+ecosνe1−e2​sinν​]
(219)

The mean anomaly is a monotonically increasing function of the true anomaly, as shown in Fig. 56. This is good because it means that MeM_eMe​ can be used in place of ν\nuν for all four quadrants on the xxx-yyy plane. If MeM_eMe​ had a peak, we would have to be concerned about which quadrant we were in.

Plugging Eq. 219 into Eq. 218 and solving for MeM_eMe​, we find:

Me=μ2h3t(1−e2)3/2M_e = \frac{\mu^2}{h^3} t \left(1 - e^2\right)^{3/2}Me​=h3μ2​t(1−e2)3/2
(220)

If we know the period of the orbit, we can simplify the equation for the mean anomaly:

Me=2πTtM_e = \frac{2\pi}{T}tMe​=T2π​t
(221)

For a circle, the mean anomaly equals the true anomaly:

νcircle=2πTt\nu_{\text{circle}} = \frac{2\pi}{T} tνcircle​=T2π​t
(222)

Further solution is not needed if the orbit is circular. Now we have the relationship between mean anomaly and time that we need for solution of these problems. Note that Eq. 203, Eq. 220, and Eq. 221 are exactly identical.

The procedure to find the eccentric anomaly is the same as in the previous section, so we can move on to deriving Kepler’s equation.

Kepler’s Equation¶

Some further trigonometry with Eq. 208, Eq. 210, and Eq. 218 yields Kepler’s Equation:

Me=E−esin⁡EM_e = E - e \sin EMe​=E−esinE
(223)

This gives the relationship between mean anomaly and eccentric anomaly. The value of MeM_eMe​ monotonically increases as a function of EEE, as shown in Fig. 57 for several values of eee.

Solution Procedures¶

Now we have all the pieces we need to apply the solution procedures discussed in the introductory section.

Given True Anomaly, Find Time Since Periapsis¶

Given a value of the true anomaly ν\nuν, the eccentric anomaly EEE can be calculated from Eq. 210. Then, Kepler’s Equation can be solved to find MeM_eMe​, and the time since periapsis is found by:

t=Me2πTt = \frac{M_e}{2\pi} Tt=2πMe​​T
(224)

Given Time Since Periapsis, Find True Anomaly¶

If, on the other hand, we are given the time since periapsis and want to find the true anomaly, we must first solve Eq. 221 for MeM_eMe​. Then we need to solve Kepler’s equation for EEE. Unfortunately, this equation is transcendental in EEE, so it cannot be solved analytically. There are several methods to solve Kepler’s equation, depending on the level of accuracy required and the access to computational tools.

Once EEE is determined, Eq. 210 can be solved for ν\nuν.

Newton’s Method to Solve Kepler’s Equation¶

For Newton’s method, we seek the roots of a function, f(E)=0f(E) = 0f(E)=0. In this case, we can rearrange Kepler’s equation as:

f(E)=E−esin⁡E−Mef(E) = E - e\sin E - M_ef(E)=E−esinE−Me​
(225)

It is also convenient to supply the derivative, when it is available, since this improves the convergence rate of the algorithm. The derivative is:

df(E)dE=f′(E)=1−ecos⁡E\frac{d f(E)}{d E} = f'(E) = 1 - e\cos EdEdf(E)​=f′(E)=1−ecosE
(226)

These equations can be provided to standard computational solvers for root finding algorithms.

Infinite Series Solutions of Kepler’s Equation¶

Although there are no analytical solutions for Kepler’s equation, several people have developed infinite series solutions. The first was developed by Lagrange:

E=Me+∑n=1∞anenE = M_e + \sum_{n = 1}^{\infty}a_n e^nE=Me​+n=1∑∞​an​en
(227)

where the coefficients ana_nan​ are given by:

an=12n−1∑k=0floor(n/2)(−1)k1(n−k)!k!(n−2k)n−1sin⁡[(n−2k)Me]a_n = \frac{1}{2^{n - 1}} \sum_{k=0}^{\mathrm{floor}(n/2)}(-1)^k\frac{1}{\left(n - k\right)!k!}\left(n - 2k\right)^{n - 1}\sin\left[\left(n - 2k\right)M_e\right]an​=2n−11​k=0∑floor(n/2)​(−1)k(n−k)!k!1​(n−2k)n−1sin[(n−2k)Me​]
(228)

and where floor(x)\mathrm{floor}(x)floor(x) means to take the next lowest integer relative to xxx.

Note: Sacchetti (2020) states that Italian astronomer Francesco Carlini was the first to prove the existence of the convergence limit for the Lagrange series five years before Laplace completed his work. DRAMA!

This infinite series converges for e<0.6627434193e < 0.6627434193e<0.6627434193, a limit that was proven by Laplace, and is therefore typically called the Laplace limit. For larger values of eee, the series diverges.

Another infinite series was developed by Bessel, which converges for all e<1e < 1e<1:

E=Me+∑n=1∞2nJn(ne)sin⁡nMeE = M_e + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n} J_n(ne)\sin n M_eE=Me​+n=1∑∞​n2​Jn​(ne)sinnMe​
(229)

where JnJ_nJn​ are the Bessel functions of the first kind, defined by their own infinite series:

Jn(x)=∑k=0∞(−1)kk!(n+k)!(x2)n+kJ_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(-1\right)^k}{k!\left(n + k\right)!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{n + k}Jn​(x)=k=0∑∞​k!(n+k)!(−1)k​(2x​)n+k
(230)

There are other feasible series solutions to Kepler’s equation, some of which are discussed by Colwell (1992).
