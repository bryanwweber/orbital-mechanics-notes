Mass, Force, and Newton’s Law of Gravitation

from pint import UnitRegistry
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

ureg = UnitRegistry()
ureg.setup_matplotlib()
ureg.formatter.default_format = "~H"
Q_ = ureg.Quantity

G = Q_(6.67430E-11, "m**3 / (kg * s**2)").plus_minus(0.00015E-11)
M = Q_(5.974E24, "kg")
R_E = Q_(6378, "km")
g_0 = (G * M / R_E**2).to("m / s**2")

In Newtonian mechanics, a net force on an object causes the object to accelerate. Forces can be transmitted between objects by direct contact or without contact by a potential field.

Examples of potential fields include gravity and static electricity. All potential fields are generated by a source. As you learned in Physics, the force due to a potential field is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between two sources in the field.

In a gravitational field, the sources are masses. Therefore, the magnitude of the force between two masses is:

F=Gm1m2r2F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}F=Gr2m1​m2​​
(1)

where m1m_1m1​ and m2m_2m2​ are the two masses, rrr is the distance between them, and GGG is Newton’s gravitational constant. Eq. 1 is called Newton’s law of gravitation, first published by Isaac Newton in the 1680s Bate et al. (1971). In SI units, GGG has the value (6.67430 ± 0.00015)×10-11 m3/(kg s2).

Due to the extremely small size of GGG, masses either have to be very large or very close together to generate a significant gravitational force.

When one of the masses is much larger than the other, we usually group the larger mass MMM with the GGG and r2r^2r2 terms:

F=m(GMr2)F = m \left(\frac{GM}{r^2}\right)F=m(r2GM​)
(2)

The second term on the right side of Eq. 2 is typically called the acceleration due to gravity:

g=GMr2g = \frac{GM}{r^2}g=r2GM​
(3)

Therefore, the weight of a smaller mass, mmm, in the gravitational field of a much larger mass is:

W=mgW = mgW=mg
(4)

For the surface of the Earth, we can plug in RE=R_E =RE​= 6378 km and M=M =M= 5.974×1024 kg to find the average acceleration due to gravity, g0=g_0 =g0​= (9.80171 ± 0.00022) m/s2.

Note

g0g_0g0​ is the average acceleration of gravity on Earth, assuming the Earth is a sphere with uniform density. In reality, Earth is a lumpy oblate spheroid, so the acceleration due to gravity varies by as much as 0.5% from the equators to the poles. In addition, using Eq. 3 does not account for the non-inertial reference frame due to the Earth’s axial rotation. Check out Wikipedia for a lot more detail!

We can also determine the variation of ggg with altitude for the Earth. Referenced to the gravity at the surface of the Earth (g0g_0g0​), we find:

gg0=1(1+z/RE)2\frac{g}{g_0} = \frac{1}{\left(1 + z/R_E\right)^2}g0​g​=(1+z/RE​)21​
(5)

where zzz is the height above the surface of the Earth. This variation is plotted in Fig. 7.

z = np.arange(0, 2000, 1) * ureg.km
g = 1/(1 + z/R_E)**2
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(z, g)
ax.set_xlabel(f"Height above Earth: {z.units}")
ax.set_ylabel("$g/g_0$")
ax.axvline(10, color="C1", label="Airplane")
ax.axvline(400, color="C2", label="International Space Station")
ax.legend();

In Fig. 7 you can see that gravity varies significantly with altitude. However, for most normal altitudes, there isn’t much variation. At 10 km, a typical airliner cruise altitude, the value of ggg is almost the same as g0g_0g0​. Even at the height of the ISS (400 km), ggg is still 90% of the value of g0g_0g0​.

Using the value of ggg at the ISS, we find that an astronaut still has a weight, mg≠0mg\neq 0mg=0. That is, the force of gravity is still acting on them. However, there is no support under their feet, so they do not feel their weight and we say they are experiencing weightlessness although they are not weightless.
