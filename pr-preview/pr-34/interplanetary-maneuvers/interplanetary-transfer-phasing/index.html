Interplanetary Transfer Phasing

Aside from the Δv\Delta vΔv requirement discussed in the previous section, the other important requirement for interplanetary trajectories is to correctly time the transfer so that the spacecraft and target planet rendezvous at arrival. Since the transfer orbits can take months or years to complete, the target planet may complete a significant fraction of its orbit during the transfer. Therefore, it is critical to launch the spacecraft at just the right time to rendezvous at arrival.

As we did before, we will use the Hohmann transfer for simplicity. However, all the techniques discussed here can be applied to non-Hohmann transfers as well, as long as the total transfer time is known.

Planetary Phase Angles¶

To correctly determine when a spacecraft should be launched, we need to know two things:

  1. The total time of the transfer orbit

  2. The angular distance between two planets, relative to the Sun

The first item can be determined from the orbital elements of the transfer orbit, so we will not discuss how to calculate it here. The second item, the angular distance between two planets, is commonly called the phase angle. This is the angle formed by drawing radii from the initial planet to the Sun and then to the final planet, as shown in Fig. 84.

In the previous section, we assumed that the orbits of the planets are circular. This allows us to define the angular distance traversed by the planet in a time interval by its average angular velocity, also called the mean motion:

(371)

Synodic Period¶

The question naturally arises, if the phase angle is γ0\gamma_0γ0​ at t=0t = 0t=0, how long does it take to become γ0\gamma_0γ0​ again? This length of time is called the synodic period of the two planets. Each pair of planets has its own synodic period, determined by the relative mean motions of the planets.

If we want the phase angle to change from γ0\gamma_0γ0​, through all the values, and come back to γ0\gamma_0γ0​, then the phase angle will have changed by 2𝜋 radians. If the initial planet has a smaller orbital radius, then the apparent motion is clockwise and the final phase angle is γ0−2π\gamma_0 - 2\piγ0​−2π. On the other hand, if the initial planet has a larger orbital radius, then the final phase angle will be γ0+2π\gamma_0 + 2\piγ0​+2π because the apparent motion is counterclockwise.

In either case, we can use Eq. 373 to calculate the time it takes for the initial phase angle to reoccur:

γ0±2π=γ0+(nf−ni)Tsyn\gamma_0 \pm 2\pi = \gamma_0 + \left(n_f - n_i\right) T_{syn}γ0​±2π=γ0​+(nf​−ni​)Tsyn​
(374)

where TsynT_{syn}Tsyn​ is the synodic period. Solving Eq. 374 for TsynT_{syn}Tsyn​ would result in two equations, depending on whether nf>nin_f > n_inf​>ni​ or vice versa. We can unify the equations by taking the absolute value of the difference nf−nin_f - n_inf​−ni​:

Tsyn=2π∣nf−ni∣T_{syn} = \frac{2\pi}{\lvert n_f - n_i\rvert}Tsyn​=∣nf​−ni​∣2π​
(375)

Finally, by plugging Eq. 371 into Eq. 375, we can write the synodic period in terms of the planets’ orbital periods, TiT_iTi​ and TfT_fTf​:

Tsyn=TiTf∣Ti−Tf∣T_{syn} = \frac{T_i T_f}{\lvert T_i - T_f\rvert}Tsyn​=∣Ti​−Tf​∣Ti​Tf​​

The synodic period is the period of the orbit of one planet relative to another, rather than relative to the Sun.
